# The Six Dimensions Framework

This framework exists to answer a single practical question: what does an adopter actually need to decide, in order to take an AI deployment from idea to scale in a public-interest context? It is not a theoretical model. It was distilled from the lived experience of deployers who have walked this path — in Maharashtra, Gujarat, Bihar, Ethiopia, and district livelihoods economies — and from the patterns that became visible only when those experiences were examined side by side.

## Why these six dimensions

A diffusion pathway has to answer two questions: what are you deploying? (the 30% — technology) and how does it get absorbed? (the 70% — everything else). Most AI deployments over-invest in the first question and under-invest in the second. The result is technically functional systems that nobody uses, or that collapse when the project champion moves on.

The 30% breaks into two independent dimensions: what you build on (your problem framing and data foundation — the choices that determine what the technology addresses) and what you build with (your models, components, and architectural patterns — the parts you assemble and must be able to swap as the technology evolves). These two are genuinely distinct. You can have the right architecture and the wrong problem. You can have the right problem and an architecture that locks you into a vendor.

The 70% breaks into four: who transforms (the deploying institution that must change how it works), who executes (the ecosystem of partners who do the work the institution should not do itself), who absorbs (the frontline people who must take the technology into their daily practice and stay more capable because of it), and what makes it last (the velocity, governance, and funding design that determines whether this runs for five years or dies after the pilot). Each of these four is genuinely independent. You can have the right ecosystem and a workforce that resists. You can have a willing workforce and a governance model that suffocates the operation.

The six dimensions are the minimum. Remove any one and you have lost a decision the adopter must make independently. The 7:14 ratio at the sub-component level — seven technology sub-components, fourteen non-technology sub-components — mirrors the 30/70 split. The structure is fractal: the same pattern repeats at every level of resolution.

## The six dimensions

### A — Problem Orientation
*Question it answers: Where do I start?*

The specific problem being solved, for whom, and why the current system does not solve it. This dimension is harder than it looks. More than half of builders who have deployed AI cannot articulate their own problem statement without help. The problem determines which data matters, which models fit, and what "good enough" looks like. If you cannot name the specific person whose life gets better and explain how, stop here. Diffusion works from the use case inward, not the product outward. This dimension also covers the data foundation you are starting from, the existing assets you can reuse, and the proof you will need to generate trust.

### B — Architecture
*Question it answers: What do I build with?*

The technology stack that keeps the deployment flexible and evolvable — models, data sovereignty, vendor independence, and the separation between reusable code (the digital public good) and the deployment instance. The frontier model race is irrelevant to most public-interest deployments. The gap is not in model capability. It is in institutional readiness to use what is already available. The architecture question is not "which model is best?" It is "how do we build something we can evolve and swap without starting over?"

### C — Institution
*Question it answers: What kind of undertaking is this?*

The deploying institution — its structures, authority, framing of the undertaking, capacity to absorb resistance, and ability to sustain the deployment when key people move on. If you frame this as a technology project, you will buy a model, run a pilot, write a report, and move on. The reframe that makes this less frightening: the 70% that is non-technology — institutional structures, policy, funding, capacity building — is familiar ground for any institution that has done a transformation project before. The AI is not the undertaking. The institutional change is.

### D — Ecosystem
*Question it answers: Who does what, and how do we work together?*

The partners, enablers, and stakeholders assembled — who they are, what roles they play, who holds the network together, and how trust is established. MahaVistaar required 25+ organisations across government departments, model providers, voice partners, funders, data providers, and domain experts. The complexity is real, but it can be made navigable: there are organisations giving paid services, organisations giving free services, and the deploying government. The question is: what are you providing, and who is accountable if it fails?

### E — Workforce
*Question it answers: How do my people absorb this — and stay capable?*

The people — field workers, extension officers, call centre staff, frontline workers — who carry the deployment into daily practice, and what they need to do that. The goal is not compliance with a new system. It is capability. AI must leave people more capable, not more dependent. The test after twelve months on the pathway: can the field worker still function if the system goes down?

### F — Operating Model
*Question it answers: What makes this last beyond the pilot?*

How the deployment sustains itself — through team structure, governance, performance tracking, and course correction. Everyone can do a pilot. A pilot has project funding, excited volunteers, and weekly check-ins. A deployment has none of those. The sustainability question, the governance design, and the transition from pilot to steady state must be answered before the pilot plan is written.
