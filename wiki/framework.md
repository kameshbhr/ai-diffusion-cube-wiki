# The Six Dimensions Framework

The Six Dimensions Framework emerged from analysing what separates AI deployments that reach scale from those that stall after pilots. It is a capture lens — when ingesting a source or creating a pathway page, these six dimensions ensure the knowledge that matters most for a next adopter is surfaced, not just the technical choices.

## Why these six dimensions

The framework's central finding is that technology accounts for roughly 30% of the effort and risk in taking an AI deployment from idea to scale. The remaining 70% sits in the five non-technology dimensions: the institution deploying, the ecosystem executing, the workforce absorbing, and the operating model sustaining. Yet most deployment documentation focuses almost entirely on the technology. The six dimensions correct for this bias — ensuring that knowledge about what happened in each domain is captured, not assumed.

The 21 sub-components (7 technology, 14 non-technology) reflect the minimum set of decisions and shifts that show up repeatedly across real deployments. Fewer would lose resolution — deployments that look similar on the surface diverge in ways that only become visible at sub-component level.

## The six dimensions

### A — Problem Orientation
*Question it answers: Where do I start?*

Before architecture or institution, a deployment needs to know exactly what problem it is solving, for whom, and why the existing system does not solve it. Problem orientation covers problem framing, data posture, existing assets, and the proof required to proceed. Getting this dimension right shapes everything downstream; most pivots and stalls trace back to a problem framing that was too broad, too generic, or not grounded in actual user constraints.

### B — Architecture
*Question it answers: What do I build with?*

The technology stack — AI models, compute, data infrastructure, interface choices — that makes the deployment work and keeps it flexible as needs evolve. Architecture decisions made early (model choice, data sovereignty, vendor dependence) are expensive to reverse later. The seven-layer architecture (user, interface, moderation, AI decision engine, knowledge and scientific models, live data sources, DPI foundation) provides a useful decomposition for planning.

### C — Institution
*Question it answers: What kind of undertaking is this?*

The institution deploying AI — its mandate, structures, authority, and capability to sustain the effort. Institutional framing (project versus transformation) determines funding, accountability, and survival through political change. Most pilots that fail at the transition to scale fail here, not in the technology.

### D — Ecosystem
*Question it answers: Who does what, and how do we work together?*

AI in public interest sectors does not deploy alone. It requires a minimum viable ecosystem of partners, data owners, knowledge institutions, and frontline delivery systems. Ecosystem design — who is in, how coordination happens, who holds the network together — is where most deployments either compound or collapse.

### E — Workforce
*Question it answers: How do my people absorb this — and stay capable?*

The field workers, extension officers, and frontline staff who carry the deployment into daily practice. Getting training timing and depth right, and testing for agency rather than dependency, determines whether the deployment amplifies human capability or replaces it. A deployment that makes workers dependent on the system creates fragility, not resilience.

### F — Operating Model
*Question it answers: What makes this last beyond the pilot?*

How the deployment sustains itself — through governance structures, funding clarity, performance measurement, and the capacity to course-correct from what the field reveals. Most pilots fail not at proof-of-concept but at the transition to steady state.
