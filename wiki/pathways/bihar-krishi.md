## Summary

Bihar Krishi is a state-wide AI agricultural advisory platform operated by Digital Green in partnership with the Government of Bihar, covering all 38 districts and reaching 850,000+ registered farmers. It is notable for two features that distinguish it from other deployments in this wiki: 25% of its registered farmer base are women, and its field rollout involved training 15,000+ extension workers before launch rather than after. It has received the ET DigiTech Gold Award 2025 and SKOCH Gold Award 2025, and demonstrates a civil society actor model for state-scale AI deployment.

## A — Problem Orientation

*What you build on.*

Bihar's agricultural advisory problem begins with a structural ratio: historically, approximately 1 extension officer for every 2,000 farmers. 🟡 This ratio is so severe that meaningful personalised advisory through the human system alone was impossible. Farmers making decisions about crop variety, fertiliser application, pest response, and scheme applications were doing so with minimal or no access to expert knowledge. The economic stakes are high: Bihar is one of India's largest agricultural states, with farming the primary livelihood for the majority of its rural population.

The deployment targeted a user base that is linguistically diverse (Hindi, Maithili, Bhojpuri are major languages), has high variance in literacy levels, and includes a significant proportion of women farmers (documented at 25% of the registered base). 🟡 Designing for women farmers — who may face additional barriers in accessing advisory services through channels designed for male-headed household heads — was a stated design consideration. How this shaped specific channel choices, content, or interface decisions is not documented beyond the registration statistic.

## C — Institution

*Who deploys AI.*

Bihar Krishi is a civil society-led deployment (Digital Green as operator) with Government of Bihar as partner. 🟡 This institutional model differs from the government-led model of MahaVistaar and the cooperative model of Amul Sarlaben — the deploying organisation is an NGO with domain expertise in agricultural extension in Bihar, not a government department.

The advantage of this model is speed and flexibility: Digital Green could hire, train, and deploy at a pace that a state government department could not match. 🟡 The risk is sustainability: civil society funding cycles create a different durability risk than government budget lines or cooperative economics.

## E — Workforce

*Who absorbs AI.*

The workforce integration at Bihar Krishi is the best-documented in this wiki relative to scale. 🟡 15,000+ extension workers across all 38 districts were trained before the platform launched. This is a deliberate sequence: training first, deployment second. The rationale — implicit in the evidence — is that extension workers who are trained before they encounter the system in the field are more likely to use it as a tool than to resist it as a replacement.

The 15,000-person training program deployed across 38 districts suggests a structured, distributed training operation of significant logistical complexity. What the training covered — whether it addressed technical use of the platform, the role of the extension worker alongside AI advice, and how to handle queries the platform could not answer — is not documented.

Whether there was resistance from extension workers who perceived the AI as a threat to their role, and what was done to address it, is not documented.

Whether extension workers can still do their job if the system is unavailable — or whether dependency has been created — is not documented.
