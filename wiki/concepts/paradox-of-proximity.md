# Paradox of Proximity

**Dimension(s):** A: Problem orientation (A1 Problem framing)
**Type:** Framework / Problem frame

## What this is

The paradox of proximity is the observation that in most district economies, the supply and demand required for economic activity — jobs and workers, services and customers, schemes and eligible citizens — exist in physical proximity but cannot find each other because the local economy is largely invisible on any digital map.

The term names a specific diagnostic claim: the problem is not that resources are absent (resource failure) or that skills are inadequate (skills gap). The problem is that things which are nearby cannot see each other. The constraint is discovery, not supply.

This reframe has a concrete operational consequence: a district facing a **local discovery failure** already has what it needs — it just cannot see it. The intervention is infrastructure, not investment. Build the discovery rails. The supply and demand will find each other.

Source: Tushar Bansal, EkStep Foundation, "Blue Dots AI — An AI Diffusion Pathway for Livelihoods," May 2026. 🟡

## Why it matters

The diagnosis determines the intervention. A "jobs crisis" or "skills gap" diagnosis leads to supply-side investment: more skilling programmes, more job fairs, more training centres. These can coexist indefinitely with the same unmet demand because they do not address the discovery constraint.

Government continuously invests in skilling and schemes; placement rates stay below 50%; service and scheme utilisation stays below 70%. 🟡 These are not failures of intent or investment — they are failures of local discovery that a supply-side diagnosis will never resolve.

The cost evidence is stark: job fairs cost ₹500+ per interaction and yield below 10% placement. The same district, with the same population, achieves above 50% placement conversion when the discovery infrastructure connects them directly — at ₹10 per voice interaction. 🟡 The constraint was never supply.

Discovery failure also does not fall evenly. It falls hardest on those with the fewest networks: the woman re-entering the workforce, the person with a disability navigating multiple barriers, the first-generation graduate without alumni connections, the daily wage worker who finds work only through whoever happens to know them. For each of them, the discovery failure is not a friction — it is the primary barrier between them and economic participation. Fixing the infrastructure changes who gets included, not through redistribution but through discovery. 🟡

## What the pathways show

🟡 **Ghaziabad (Uttar Pradesh, India):** Fewer than 10% of SMBs surfaced on a shared digital map made over 10,000 local job openings visible in under 60 days. National platforms showed fewer than 100 local listings for the same district. The jobs had always existed. They were digitally dark.

🟡 **Ghaziabad vignette (from source document, verbatim):** "A commerce graduate named Kavita is being urged by her family to move to Delhi — convinced a career cannot be built at home. A manufacturing firm two kilometres from her house is looking for someone exactly like her. Neither knows the other exists." The document presents this as a pattern repeating across sectors and across every district.

🟡 **The structural source of digital darkness:** National platforms were built for digitally fluent users, not for the fragmented, vernacular, hyperlocal reality of district life. The long tail of SMBs, local talent, service providers, and citizens — the majority of any district's economic activity — is largely invisible. This is not a niche gap. It is the untapped core.

🟡 **The economic case (Ghaziabad):** A conservative 5% improvement in workforce participation across youth, women, and persons with disabilities adds 70,000 workers to the local economy. They earn ₹1 lakh per year, with 67% retained in the district — ₹700 crore in local spending. Through a 1.5x local multiplier, total GDP addition reaches ₹1,050 crore per year for one district. Scaled across India's top 100 progressive districts: ₹87,500 crore annually. This excludes welfare scheme utilisation gains, SMB productivity improvements, and services activation. 🟡

🟡 **SMBs as the hidden majority:** SMBs contribute roughly 70% of employment but only 30% of GDP in India. This productivity gap is partly a discovery gap — SMBs that cannot find talent, customers, or supply cannot grow. 🟡
