# Dimension 3: Institution

**One-line:** Who deploys AI — the deploying institution's structures, authority, capabilities, and how it secures and sustains funding.

---

## What this dimension covers

The institution is the organisation that takes ownership of the deployment — not just the technical implementation, but the mandate, the resources, and the accountability.

- **C1 — Framing:** How the institution frames AI internally — as tool or threat, as augmentation or replacement, as experiment or commitment. Different institutional actor types need different framings.
- **C2 — Resistance and non-transferability:** What forms of institutional resistance emerge, and what makes deployment experience non-transferable when key staff move on. Resistance is rational, not obstructive. Non-transferability is structural, not a failure.
- **C3 — Institutional knowledge:** How the institution captures and retains what it learns. The specific leadership act: authorising the institution's knowledge to be made publicly queryable.

## Key patterns across pathways

**C1 — Framing the undertaking:**
⬜ The reframe that makes AI deployment less frightening for institutions: the 70% of the work that is non-technical (institutional structures, policy, funding, capacity building) is familiar ground for any government that has done a transformation project before. It has nothing to do with AI.

⬜ Different institutional actors need different framings:
- **System declarant** (Chief Secretary, PMO): needs vision and possibility — what could this do for the people I serve?
- **Evaluator** (department head): needs feasibility and options — can I do this, and what are the trade-offs?
- **Constructor** (CTO, programme lead): needs specifications.
- **Agent of change** (extension officer, district official): needs operational guidance.
The pathway must frame the undertaking differently for each.

⬜ Simulation, not documentation, is how institutional imagination is activated. Not workshops where experts lecture. Simulated experiences where the adopting institution's own people encounter decision points and trade-offs in a compressed timeframe, using their own data and context.

⬜ Bounded ambition is a framing strategy: "run a bounded pilot in Kharif crops in selected districts" has fundamentally different institutional appetite from "transform state agriculture."

**C2 — Resistance and non-transferability:**
🟡 Bureaucratic inertia is not stupidity. Officials who resist AI adoption are often protecting something real: discretion, authority, familiar processes that work well enough. Automated systems remove the ability to grant or deny on a case-by-case basis. For officials whose authority comes from that discretion, AI is a threat. This resistance is rational, and pretending otherwise is naive.

🟡 One way to dissolve resistance: reframe from "officials blocking AI" to "the system failing to reach people." When you show an agriculture secretary that their own scheme portals are structurally inaccessible to the farmers they were built for (requires smartphone, reliable internet, literacy, time, correct language), resistance often shifts. The problem is not AI. The problem is that the department's own knowledge doesn't reach the people it was meant for. AI is the solution, not the threat.

🟡 Non-transferability operates across institutional types. Bihar Krishi was built independently, with different partners, under a different institutional logic. A cooperative learns differently from a state department, which learns differently from a national ministry. Past success creates confidence but not readiness — these are different things. Each new technology requires its own cycle of learning, trust-building, and institutional adaptation.

⬜ The interventions for resistance and non-transferability are different: resistance requires narrative reframing; non-transferability requires a new learning cycle. Both are reasons the adopter's institution is not ready, but they must not be conflated.

**C3 — Institutional knowledge:**
🟡 When the Department of Agriculture, Maharashtra, authorised its advisory corpus to flow through MahaVISTAAR, it made an institutional knowledge claim: "this is what we know, and we certify it." That is the act of institutionalising knowledge by making it publicly queryable. A specific leadership act is required: authorise your institution's knowledge to be made accessible — and stand behind what it says.

🟡 AI reveals what knowledge the institution lacks. In MahaVISTAAR, 205,000 crop/pest queries per month create a visible, quantified demand signal for knowledge that was previously tacit. Where farmers' questions go unanswered, the institution now sees its own gaps.

🟡 The test for whether institutional knowledge has been embedded (not just held by individuals): can the system function when every individual who helped build it has moved to a different role? In Indian government, extension officers rotate routinely. If knowledge stops at the first layer, it will not survive the second posting cycle.

## Key decisions an adopter faces

| Decision | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| How to frame the undertaking internally? | Frame by actor type. System declarants need vision; evaluators need options; constructors need specs; agents of change need operational guidance. Never use one framing for all. |
| How to respond to resistance? | Treat it as rational, not obstructive. Reframe from "officials blocking AI" to "your department's knowledge not reaching the people it was built for." |
| How to handle the new-technology learning cycle? | Each technology requires its own cycle. Don't assume that because the institution managed one digital transformation, it is ready for the next. Provide a new learning pathway. |
| What leadership act is needed for institutional knowledge? | A named leader must authorise the institution's knowledge to be publicly queryable — and stand behind what it says. This cannot be delegated to a technical team. |
| How to build knowledge beyond the first person? | Who trains the trainers? Can a district official explain why the AI recommended what it did to a sceptical colleague? Design knowledge transfer depth that survives staff rotation. |

## Common failure modes

- **Framing as a technology project.** "Buy a model, run a pilot, write a report, move on." The 70% that is institutional transformation work is invisible if the framing is wrong.
- **Treating resistance as obstruction.** Officials protecting discretion and authority are behaving rationally. Treating their resistance as the problem prevents seeing the real problem (knowledge not reaching the people it was meant for).
- **Assuming non-transferability won't apply.** "We did a similar project last year" does not mean the institution is ready for this one. Each new technology requires its own learning cycle.
- **Knowledge in one person's head.** If the deployment depends on one expert who understands how it works, the institution has not learned. The test: can the system function when that person moves to a different role?
- **No named leader who stands behind the knowledge.** AI advisory without institutional attribution is advice without authority. A senior official must authorise the knowledge corpus and stand behind it.

## Gaps

- C1 framing: no pathway documents the internal process by which a specific institution shifted from "technology project" framing to "institutional change project" framing — what specifically triggered the reframe.
- C2 resistance: the most under-documented dimension in provider contributions. Providers are reluctant to discuss internal resistance. Seek field sources that name specific resistance episodes and how they were resolved.
- C2 non-transferability: documented by type (cooperative vs. state department vs. ministry) but not by specific mechanism — what exactly cannot be transferred and why.
- C3: the process of building the advisory corpus (what content, how validated, by whom) is not documented for any deployment in current sources.
